How we measure a shot

Last updated October 31, 2026

Every figure on a shot is read from the club's video. This page explains each one: where on the sheet it is measured, which camera sees it, and how far to trust it. Where a figure could not be measured well enough, it shows a dash (–) rather than a guess.

In the drawings the thrower is at the bottom, as on a game's page. Distances along the sheet are to scale. Across the sheet they are stretched, so that differences of a few inches show. The rock in them is a made-up example.

The cameras

Each sheet has four cameras. A long camera hangs behind each end and looks down the whole sheet. For any throw, the one at the far end faces the thrower: it sees the slide, the release, the hog line and the rock's first few seconds. The one behind the thrower sees the rock run down to the far house and the skip's broom. An overhead camera above each house looks straight down on it, which is the best view of where stones come to rest.

The long cameras are measured against the paint on the ice: the rings, the hog lines, the centre line, the four-foot strip and the sidelines. Checked against that paint, they place a stone across the sheet to within about an inch, from the hack to well past the hog line.

Slide, Release and Final at the broom

Three straight lines are fitted through the rock's path, each over a different stretch of the delivery, and each is followed down the sheet to the skip's broom. Where it crosses the broom's depth says how far it was from the broom, and on which side.

  • Slide is where the slide was aimed. It is fitted from 9 ft behind the tee (halfway between the hack and the back line, once the push is over) to the release point.
  • Release is where the release sent the rock. It is fitted over the first 9 ft past the release point.
  • Final is the line the rock was running on once it was free. It is fitted over the first 9 ft past the hog line. This is the figure in the Line column of a practice session's table.

All three are read from the long camera at the far end, which sees every frame of the delivery (30 a second).

Wide or narrow. Wide is the side away from the way the rock curled; narrow is the side it curled toward. A rock that did not curl either way is said to be left or right of the broom. A line within 2 in of the broom reads "On the broom" on a game's page and "to the broom" on a practice card, where the figures are in whole inches.

From the slide. Under Release, "+8″ from the slide" says the release sent the rock 8 inches wider than the slide was aimed (− is narrower). It is shown only when the difference is more than twice the two lines' combined uncertainty, so a figure there is a real change, not noise.

When a line shows a dash. Each line knows its own uncertainty at the broom, worked out from how closely the stone's path follows it. A Slide or Release line whose uncertainty at the broom is more than 6 in is not shown. Neither is shown when the release point was not read. Typically they are good to about 2 in.

On brooms well off the centre line, the slide usually points outside the broom, and the rock curls back in. That is what the rocks did: it holds against the paint, the overhead cameras and the broom itself.

Release point

The moment the thrower's hand comes off the handle. A small model looks at the stone in every frame of the long camera at the far end, starting half a metre behind the tee, and says whether the hand is still on the handle; the release is where that answer changes and stays changed. On the delivery chart the dots before it are navy and those after it gold. Checked against releases marked by hand, it lands within half a metre on 18 throws out of 20, typically within 8 cm.

Hack, and At the hog line

Hack is the hack the player throws from, Left or Right, read from where their stones sit before the push across the whole game. Under it is where this rock's stone was set, measured from the centre line.

At the hog line compares the rock, as its final line crosses the hog line, with a straight line from the middle of that player's hack to the broom. "On the line" means within 2 in.

Weight

The time from the rock crossing the near hog line to it crossing the far one, in seconds. Each long camera sees the hog line at the far end of the sheet painted across its picture, so it times the crossing as the stone passes over the paint. A rock that never reached the far hog line has no weight. It says "estimated" when one of the crossings had to be worked out from the rock's speed rather than seen, usually because players were in the way.

Rotation

How fast the thrower set the rock turning, in turns a minute (rpm). The long camera at the far end follows the rock from 8 m past the tee, where the hand is off, and a second small model finds the two ends of the handle in each frame, ten times a second. As the stone turns, the handle swings across it once a turn; the rpm is how often it swings.

The rpm is the rate over the first 1¼ turns: the spin as thrown, before the rock slows. Within that it agrees with careful frame-by-frame counts to about 10%. A rock seen turning under half a turn, usually a fast hit, is given as a range, such as 3–5 rpm. Under about a third of a turn there is no reading.

In-turn or out-turn comes from which way the rock curled and which hack the player throws from: the handle's swing looks the same either way round.

Curl

How far the rock ended up from its final line: the final line is followed on to where the rock stopped, or to where it first hit a stone, and the distance across is the curl. The long camera behind the thrower follows the rock all the way down. The rock is said to have curled one way or the other only when that distance is 5 cm or more.

Came to rest

Where the rock stopped, from the overhead camera above the house: which ring it is in, and how far it is from the button. The overhead camera sees the house from directly above, so this is the most exact figure on the page, good to a few centimetres.

Thinking time

How long a team took to decide on a shot. As in a timed game, the clock runs while the team is deciding and stops once the stone is on its way: from 5 seconds after the previous stone comes to rest (time to clear the ice), until this stone crosses the tee line at the throwing end. An end's first stone is not timed, because there is no stone before it to start the clock, and neither is a stone thrown after one that never came to rest.

Sensor short and long

On a practice session, these are the times your own timing app sent for the throw, shown as it sent them. They are not measured from the video, and are paired with the throw the cameras saw at the same moment.
